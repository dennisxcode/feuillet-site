The rule the product follows

Students get a private workspace. Parents get the marks, worked out.

A planner nobody trusts is a planner nobody writes in. So what a student plans stays theirs, and what parents and staff see is the school's own data, made legible. Ticks are never reported to anyone, which also means nobody can game them.

Private Only the student sees

  • What they have ticked off, and when
  • Their own notes, captures and plans
  • How they broke an exam into study blocks
  • Anything they added that is not school work

Shared Parents and staff see

  • Marks the portal already publishes, plus the weighted average, the trend since the last étape and standing against the class average
  • Work the portal reports as not handed in
  • Counts per level, for staff, with no names attached

Data and Loi 25

Compliance you can check, not a promise.

The portal password never leaves the student's Mac

It is encrypted into the machine's own keychain. Our servers never receive it and could not use it. Reading the portal happens on the student's computer, not ours.

A written agreement, adapted to Loi 25

Provided before a pilot begins, naming what is collected, why, for how long and who may access it.

Highest privacy by default

Nothing is shared with anyone until a school or a student turns it on. Loi 25 requires that default; here it is also the design.

Leaving takes everything with you

A student can export their data and delete their account themselves. A school ending a pilot ends the access at the same moment.

Students who struggle to plan

Built by, and for, someone who forgets the project until the night before.

Writing the work down, planning when to do it, and noticing what has been sitting untouched are exactly the skills a school currently teaches one student at a time, in person. Feuillet does the mechanical half of that for every student at once: work arrives already written down, a project says how many days it has been ignored, and an exam becomes study spread across the days that remain.

It is an organization tool. Not a service, not a diagnosis, and not a replacement for the people who provide either.

Why it should work

The mechanisms are evidenced. Feuillet is not, yet.

d = 1.05

Coaching students to write the work down and plan it improves planning that much, with homework completion at d = .85. The intervention is a person sitting with one student every week: checking the agenda was filled in, that the materials exist, that a plan was made.

In FeuilletThat check happens by itself, for every student. Work arrives from the portal already written down, and it lands on the class period it belongs to instead of on a list.

Langberg et al., HOPS randomized trial, parent-rated. Teacher ratings did not move significantly in the same study.

g = 0.83

Organizational skills are teachable, across twelve controlled studies of 1,054 students, with inattention improving at g = 0.56. The gains come from structure imposed from outside, not from a student trying harder.

In FeuilletThe structure is the product: one week, one place, every source folded in. A student who never opens a planner still has an organized week, because they did not have to build it.

Bikic et al., meta-analysis of organizational skills interventions, 2017.

d = 0.65

Deciding when and where beats deciding to. Across 94 tests, people who fixed the moment and the place of a task were far likelier to actually do it than people who only intended to.

In FeuilletNothing is ever only a title and a due date. Every travail sits on a day and a period, and an exam becomes study blocks on specific evenings, which is that decision made in advance.

Gollwitzer & Sheeran, meta-analysis of implementation intentions, 94 tests, over 8,000 participants.

2 of 10

Only two of ten common study techniques earned a high-utility rating in the field's standard review: spreading study across days, and testing yourself. Rereading and highlighting, what most students actually do, did not.

In FeuilletAn exam asks for its chapters once, then the hours are spread across the days that remain and reminders count down to them. The app never proposes a cram session, because the evidence says it is the wrong one.

Dunlosky et al., Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013.

38,529

Procrastination predicts lower grades, across 33 studies and that many students, and the link is stronger in younger students than in university ones. Secondary school is where it costs the most.

In FeuilletA project counts the days it has sat untouched and says so, on a widening interval. It is the one thing a paper agenda and a checkbox both physically cannot do.

Kim & Seo, Personality and Individual Differences, 2015.

What none of this says. No study has measured Feuillet. These findings support the mechanisms it is built on, not the product, and anyone claiming otherwise on a page like this is selling you something. A pilot is how it gets measured: pick the question, and the school gets the result whichever way it comes out.

The pilot

Eight weeks, one group, no cost.

01

Pick a small group

One class or one level. Students who want to try a different way of organizing their work, not a mandated rollout.

02

Ask one narrow question

Chosen with you before it starts. Are these students still checking their week in week eight? Do fewer travaux get handed in late?

03

We handle setup

The portal connects itself, so the school adds no data entry and no maintenance. Expect one meeting and one email to the group.

04

You get the answer in writing

Grouped patterns only, with consent, and the honest result. If it did not work, that is the report.
